Commercial pressure washing is mostly about the water

Anyone can rent a machine and clean a walk. What sets a commercial job apart is what happens to the water afterward.

Parking lots collect oil and tire residue. Dumpster pads collect food waste and grease. Gas station aprons collect fuel. Wash any of that and the runoff carries it along, straight to a storm drain that leads to a creek without treatment.

Property managers often assume this is the contractor's problem. City rules speak to whoever causes the discharge, so it is worth settling in writing who collects the water and where it goes.

What Dallas, Fort Worth and Frisco actually say

The three cities use different words, and all of them name mobile power washing or pavement washing. Each is a short summary, so read the source before you rely on it.

Dallas

City code gives a defense for water used on streets, walks, lots and driveways if it holds no soap, detergent or similar cleaner. It separately prohibits mobile power washing and pavement washdown when the wastewater holds a harmful quantity of cleaner. The person discharging carries the burden of proof.

Fort Worth

Mobile commercial cleaners need a yearly permit, $25 for the business and $25 per wash unit. Wash water must not leave the property or enter a storm drain except under a narrow cold water exemption. Illicit discharge fines can reach $2,000 per day per offense.

Frisco

The stormwater ordinance names mobile power washer wastewater with soap or other harmful cleaners, and pavement wastewater where a spill was not removed. Service station pavement wash water must pass through a grease, oil and sand interceptor first.

What a property manager should ask for

Put this in the quote

  • Which areas are included, by square footage if possible
  • Hot or cold water, and which cleaning products
  • Who blocks storm drains and collects the water
  • Where the collected water goes
  • What hours the work happens, and how walkways stay safe

Warning signs

  • No plan for soapy water beyond "it goes in the drain"
  • "Biodegradable, so it is fine." Fort Worth says biodegradable detergents can still kill fish
  • No certificate of insurance
  • In Fort Worth, no city registration number on the rig

Parking lots, walks and entries

Before washing a lot, oil spots should be dealt with first. Fort Worth's best practice guide says to absorb puddles with clay, bag the clay and place it in a dumpster. Then the storm inlets can be screened and the water contained.

For sidewalks and entries, timing is the concern. Wet concrete by a door is a slip risk, so most managers book early mornings or closed hours. Ask the company what it recommends for your hours. The same advice on staining and old concrete applies here as on a driveway. Retail centers and offices across the metroplex can call the line from any of the cities it covers.

Setting a cleaning schedule for a site

No city rule sets how often a storefront or lot must be washed. It depends on traffic, spills and how the site looks. Ask the company to walk the property, and suggest a schedule for each area, such as entries and dumpster pads more often than the full lot. Then have the schedule written with the wash water plan, so each visit follows the same rules. Keeping that record helps too, since Dallas city code puts the burden of proving a discharge falls within a defense on the person making it.
