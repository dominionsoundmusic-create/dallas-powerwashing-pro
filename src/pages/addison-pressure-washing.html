Pressure washing in a town built for restaurants

Addison is small, at 4.4 square miles, but the town says it holds more than 180 restaurants, 22 hotels and over 12 million square feet of office space.

That mix makes pressure washing in Addison different from a typical suburb. Most of what gets washed here is someone's place of business: patios along Belt Line Road, dumpster pads, hotel entries, office walks and parking garages. Customers notice dirty concrete, and greasy concrete by a door is a slip hazard.

It also means that wash water here tends to carry more than dust. Food grease and cooking oil are exactly what storm drain rules are written for.

Who is responsible for what

Restaurant tenants

Patios and the area right outside the door, depending on the lease. Read yours.

Landlords and managers

Shared walks, parking and dumpster enclosures are often theirs. Agree in writing who books the work.

Townhome owners

Around Addison Circle and similar communities, an HOA may handle fronts and walks. Check first.

Addison's Chapter 72 and your wash water

The town added Chapter 72 to its code in 2010. It is not written with power washers in mind, and that is why you should ask careful questions.

The ordinance bars pollutants, or water containing pollutants, from the storm drain system when they violate water quality standards. Its list of exempt discharges includes individual residential vehicle washing, but it does not mention pavement washing or mobile washing services. It also exempts any other water source not containing pollutants, which is why greasy water is the real question. Conviction can bring a fine of up to $500, each day counts as a separate offense, and the town can also order remedies.

A sensible reading is to treat greasy wash water as something that can not go down the drain. Absorb grease first, block the inlet, and collect the water. Fort Worth's published best practices give a clear model for that, even though the rule itself is a different city's.

Booking around business hours

Think about deliveries as well, since a truck in the way can cost a crew an hour.

Most Addison properties will want the job done when the doors are closed. Say so when you call, along with these details:

  • The surface: pavers, stamped concrete or plain concrete
  • What is on it: grease, gum, spilled drinks or plain dirt
  • Where the nearest storm inlet is
  • Whether a water spigot is available
  • Who the invoice goes to

Pavers and sealed patios need gentler handling than plain concrete, so mention them. For homes in town, see house washing. Costs are covered in the cost guide.

Garages, floor drains and hotel entries

With this many offices and hotels, a lot of Addison washing happens under a roof or on a ramp. That is where people get caught out. Fort Worth's guidance warns that most outdoor floor drains connect to the storm sewer, not the sanitary sewer, so do not assume a drain in a garage or dock leads to treatment. Have your building engineer confirm where it goes before anyone starts.

Hotel and office entries raise a different concern, which is people. A wet entry at 7 a.m. is a slip risk when the first guests walk through. Ask the company for cones, a work sequence that leaves one door dry, and a finish time that suits your front desk. The City of Dallas flyer on wash water recommends cones wherever vehicles or people are present, and that is sensible here too. Addison buys its water from Dallas, so if Dallas is in a drought stage, ask the town what applies before you schedule a large job.
