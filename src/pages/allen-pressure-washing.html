Allen: a railroad stop that became a city of 100,000

Allen began as a railroad town in 1870, took its name in 1872, and got a railroad water supply stop on Cottonwood Creek around 1876. It was still a few hundred people when it incorporated in 1953. Then it grew: 1,940 residents in 1970, 18,309 in 1990, 43,554 in 2000 and 104,627 in the 2020 census. Allen's history is still on display at the Allen Heritage Center in the old railroad depot, but most of its homes went up from the 1980s on.

For pressure washing, that means Allen is mostly family neighborhoods with brick and stone fronts, backyard pools and long fence lines, plus busy retail around Watters Creek and the outlet center. Backyards first: pool decks, patios and fences

With a pool in the yard, the backyard is often the bigger job. Pool decks gather pollen, sunscreen and leaves; covered patios and outdoor kitchens collect grease near the grill; fences gray in the sun. Each needs its own approach:

Pool decks

Pressure suited to the deck coating, and runoff kept out of the water. Ask how the company protects the pool.

Patios and kitchens

A surface cleaner on the slab, degreaser by the grill, and care around fans and lights on the patio cover.

Fences and decks

Wood cleaner and a gentle rinse, then dry days before stain. Composite decking follows the maker's rules, such as Trex's limits on pressure.

Water and runoff in Allen

Allen joined the North Texas Municipal Water District in 1998, the district's history shows. When the district or the city declares drought stages, outdoor water use can be restricted, so check the City of Allen's current rules in a dry summer. Wherever you are in DFW, rinse water should not carry soap or cleaners to the storm drain; the City of Dallas's guidance, for example, says to block the drain and collect water when chemicals are used.
