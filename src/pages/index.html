Why washing in Dallas is its own job

Plenty of pressure washing advice is written for other parts of the country. Four things make North Texas different.

Clay under the concrete

Much of the area sits on Blackland Prairie clay, which shrinks and swells as it dries and wets. Driveway slabs crack and tilt, and a wand held too close at a raised edge can chip what is already weak.

Pollen most of the year

Children's Health lists cedar in winter, tree pollen from February to June, grass into September and ragweed into November. Mold also peaks from July through late summer, and shaded walls that stay damp can turn green.

Heat that dries soap on walls

The first 100 degree day at DFW comes on July 1 on average, and the last on August 27. Cleaner that dries on hot brick or siding can streak, so summer jobs tend to run early in the day.

Rules on where the water goes

Storm drains here run to creeks and lakes without treatment. Dallas, Fort Worth and Frisco all address power washers by name in their city rules, and drought plans can limit outdoor washing.

Fences and decks in the Dallas heat

Wood takes the most careful hand. Softwood such as cedar and pine marks easily, so fence cleaning and deck cleaning call for low pressure and a wide fan tip. HGTV puts softwood at roughly 500 to 600 PSI for decks, far below what a concrete job uses.

If a contractor plans to use the same setting on your cedar fence as on your driveway, that is your cue to ask why.

What to ask before you say yes

  • Can I see a certificate of insurance?
  • What pressure and tip will you use on each surface?
  • If you use soap, how will you keep it out of the storm drain?
  • Is the price for the whole job, and is it in writing?
  • Is my home old enough that lead paint is a concern?

This site found no Texas state license for power washing, so these questions do the job a license number would do in other trades. Read how the line works for what happens after you call.
